p(doom) calculator · submissions report · export of 15 Oct 2026

The busiest day did not repeat, and the visitors who submit fastest are the ones who keep the page's number.

424 anonymous submissions between 5 October 2025 and 14 October 2026. The calculator asks each visitor for three conditional probabilities and multiplies them: that powerful AI arrives, that it then behaves dangerously, and that the dangerous behaviour then becomes a global catastrophe. The product is that visitor's p(doom). The last report was built on the evening of 1 October, the site's busiest day. This one adds the fortnight after it: 59 rows, one of them the tail of that evening and 58 spread over the next 13 days, never more than nine in a day. Their median is 54%, against 23% for 1 October and 49% for September, so the burst was a crowd passing through, not a change in who visits. Two patterns are new in this export. The page has recorded since 12 September the moment it set the sliders to the quiz's proposal, and 164 rows now say how long the visitor then spent before pressing submit: of those who submitted within a minute, 63% kept the proposal; of those who took more than three minutes, 14%. And split by quiz level, the monthly median shows something the single line hides: the expert line has fallen three months running, from 59% in August to 23% in October.

One thing has to be read before any figure below. A visitor who takes a quiz is not handed neutral sliders: the page computes a starting p(doom) from their answers and sets all three sliders to it. 106 of 314 quiz-takers (identical repeat rows aside) registered that proposed number without moving a midpoint. Those rows are the page's arithmetic, so wherever submissions are drawn one by one, the strips and the proposed-start chart, they are the round dots and the others are arrows, and the figures that matter are given for the other rows separately. Two words carry the split: kept means the three midpoints are exactly where the quiz put them, and moved means at least one is not. Kept does not mean nothing was touched: the uncertainty band is a separate control, and most kept rows changed it. Moved does not mean free of the proposal either; a visitor who drags a slider from 72% to 60% may have got there only because 72% was in front of them.

The unit is a submission, not a person, except where a signature says otherwise. Since 19 August the page signs each submission with a per-browser key, and 253 of the 256 rows since then carry one, so repeat rows from one browser can be told apart from two people agreeing. Before that date nothing distinguishes them.

    The proposed start320 quiz submissions · 164 timed

    A third of quiz-takers registered the number the page proposed, and they registered it quickly

    After a quiz the page does not hand the visitor neutral sliders. It proposes a p(doom) from the answers, an opinion question picks a band and the share of boxes ticked moves the number up within it, and sets all three factors to the cube root of the proposal. It is tempting to read the quiz as a survey taken alongside the sliders. It is not: it is an input to them, and a visitor who answers it and presses submit has registered the page's arithmetic rather than their own. Every quiz submission is therefore split from here on into kept, where no midpoint was moved from the proposal, and moved. This export adds two views of that split: where each kind of row lands on the scale, and how long the visitor looked at the proposal before submitting.

    Dunning–Kruger86 check-takers · 122 medium self-ratings

    Do the visitors who know least claim most? Partly in what they tick, not in how sure they are

    The Dunning–Kruger effect is the finding that people with the least skill in an area overestimate it the most, because the skill they lack is also the skill needed to judge it. Testing it needs three things from the same people: a measure of their skill that cannot be gamed, their own estimate of that skill, and ideally a measure of how certain they are. The site has partial versions of each. Clicking the expert card is a claim of expertise, and the 30-term check, with its fifteen decoys, scores the claim. The medium quiz asks visitors to rate their own skill with system prompts, which is the closest thing on the site to its definition of an expert, hands-on experience, and separately asks which vulnerabilities they know. And every visitor sets an uncertainty band on each slider, which is how sure they say they are. What the site cannot do is the classic test. Nobody is asked to estimate their own score, and only people who claimed expertise are scored at all, so a visitor who underrates themselves, the other half of the effect, never takes the check. This section does not draw the familiar Dunning–Kruger chart, self-estimate against actual score by quartile, because that chart takes the same shape from random numbers (Nuhfer and colleagues, 2016; Gignac and Zajenkowski, 2020). It compares within people instead, on three questions.

    What the data says

    Fifteen things this export supports

    • The busiest day was a one-off, and the fortnight after it looks like September. 58 submissions from 2 to 14 October, 4.5 a day against September's 4.3, at a median of 54% against September's 49%. 1 October, now 37 rows at 23% with one late arrival counted, is still nearly twice any other day. Two later days with five or more rows had medians under 25%, so the low tail is still there, but at the rate of a few rows a week rather than 33 in an evening.
    • Visitors who submit quickly keep the page's number; visitors who take their time move it. 63% of the rows submitted within a minute of the proposal kept it, against 14% of those that took more than three minutes, on 40 and 21 rows of the 164 that record the time. The pattern holds inside each level. Among movers, time does not predict how far they move. This is the first measurement the site has of how a proposal is received, and it cannot say whether quick keepers agree or are in a hurry.
    • A mover's commonest landing place is under 10%. 44 of 208 movers registered less than 10%, more than in any other band of ten points, from proposals with a median of 64%. Only 2 of 106 kept rows are that low. The share has sat between 15% and 20% of movers in every period except 1 October, when it was 41%. The movers' median sits between two kinds of visitor: those who adjust the proposal and those who reject it.
    • Split by level, the pooled monthly median hides a falling expert line. On every row the expert median went 59%, 32%, 23% from August to October; beginner went 72%, 48%, 56%; medium 69%, 63%, 40%. Among movers the levels converged in September at 31%, 27% and 28%, and in October medium and expert went on down to 20% and 22% while 16 beginner movers registered 54%. The beginner figure is small and partly near-keepers; the falling expert line is the steadier of the two.
    • The page proposes a number, and 34% of quiz-takers register it with the midpoints unchanged. 106 of 314 quiz submissions, identical repeats excluded. Their medians by level, 72%, 81% and 70%, are the formula's output. Of the 208 visitors who did move a midpoint, 168 moved down; the median move across all of them is 29 points down, and among those who moved down it is 37. The kept share has fallen to 24% from 2 to 14 October, mostly on the expert quiz, where 1 of 15 kept. What "kept" cannot say is that nothing was touched: 52 of the 55 kept rows that stored the proposed band changed it.
    • Once the proposal is taken out, the three quiz levels land close together over the whole export. Pooled medians: beginner 55%, medium 63%, expert 38%. Among visitors who set their own numbers, repeats excluded: 36%, 33% and 31%, on 54, 75 and 79 rows. The spread the pooled figures show is the three quizzes proposing different numbers and being kept at different rates, 42%, 39% and 20%.
    • The readers who passed the vocabulary check expect powerful AI, and discount the step from danger to catastrophe more than anyone else. Their chain runs 90%, 80%, 50% among the 60 who set their own numbers. No other quiz group goes that low on the last step: beginners who set their own leave it at 73%, medium takers at 75%, self-declared experts at 93%. Only the visitors from before any quiz existed sit there too, at 50%. Two things could produce it, and this export cannot separate them: a considered view, or an expert quiz that asks about mechanisms and never once mentions an outcome.
    • Passing the vocabulary check goes with a lower number than claiming expertise did. Pooled, 32% (n = 74) against 68% (n = 22). Among visitors who set their own number the gap is 25% against 44%. That is a different comparison, not a decomposition: it leaves out the kept rows on both sides, and so changes who is compared as well as what. Neither figure isolates the proposal's effect, and the two groups are months apart.
    • The drop in names recognised by verified experts was the 1 October crowd. The 18 verified that day recognised a median 2 of the 12 names in the field; the 14 verified since recognise a median 4, the same as the 34 verified before 20 September. Within the expert quiz it is the campaign organisations that go with the number: a median 54% for those who recognise two or three, 23% for those who recognise none.
    • Visitors who spot fewer real AI terms are twice as likely to claim a fake one; they are not more certain. Of the check-takers who spotted 12 or fewer of the 15 real terms, 8 of 24 ticked a decoy, against 5 of 32 who spotted 13 or 14 and 5 of 30 who spotted all 15. That is the overclaiming half of the Dunning–Kruger effect. The certainty half is absent: among expert-quiz takers the check score is unrelated to the band they set (ρ = −0.07), and the medium quiz's self-rated system-prompt experts owe their narrow bands to the page's formula. The 13 expert-quiz takers who ticked a decoy registered a median 56% against 25%, a gap a permutation test puts at p ≈ 0.07 on so few people. Nobody is asked to estimate their own score, so the classic test cannot be run.
    • The expert gate is a recommendation, and three people have now declined it. 86 finished the check: 74 scored 26 or better and went through, nine scored below and took the quiz it recommended, and three scored 24 or 25 and continued to the expert quiz anyway, registering 27%, 0.4% and 10%. The expert share since the check is 30% against 33% before it, and 26% in the fortnight after 1 October.
    • How many boxes a visitor ticks does not sort the number they set, at any level, and the orderings keep changing. Among movers, from the third that ticked most to the third that ticked fewest: beginner 40%, 40%, 31%; medium 35%, 22%, 49%; expert 42%, 20%, 31%. The medium ordering is different at every export. Two single lists hold their direction across exports: the beginner catastrophe list, inversely (25%, 47%, 52% among movers), and the expert campaign-organisations list.
    • What a medium-quiz taker recognises has no measurable association with the number they choose. The vulnerability checklist is ρ = +0.18 across all rows and −0.02 among the 75 who set their own number, repeats excluded, down from +0.12 at the last report. Having seen AI go off the rails is +0.07 among movers. What still correlates is the three opinion questions, which is close to tautological: they ask about competition, governance and reaction speed, and they also choose the band the proposal starts in.
    • P(powerful AI) is rated highest of the three links and the extremes are used. Its median is 88% against 81% and 80% for the two conditionals, yet 76 of 424 submissions (18%) put it at 50% or below. 18 submissions put the final number at exactly 0% and 14 at 99% or above; the p10–p90 band has a median width of 35 points. One of the zeros and three of the 99%-or-above rows are new since the last report. The band is not an interval around the midpoint: the page draws 4,000 samples per factor from a bell curve centred on the midpoint with a standard deviation of half the slider's ± spread, rejects draws outside 0 to 1, multiplies, and takes the 10th and 90th percentiles. Truncation near 0 or 1 skews that distribution, so the midpoint product can sit outside the band, and on 133 of the 424 submissions it does. Before 20 August 2026 the draws were uniform across each band; rows from before then keep the band they were submitted with.
    • Impatient double-submits have stopped. Zero identical repeat rows among the 222 since the 2 September fix, against six in the fortnight before it. Eleven browsers did submit more than once since, and all eleven changed something in between; the newest took the beginner quiz three times in six and a half minutes on 2 October with the same answers and three different slider settings, which is a person using the site rather than a button being pressed twice.

    What to change next: the page can now time a decision, and should count what happens inside it

    Each report ends with the changes the data argues for, and marks what happened to the ones before it. Nothing that changes what the page collects has shipped since the 1 October report, so the four measured items are the same four, measured again on 59 more rows, and all four still hold. One item is new, and it comes from the time-on-sliders finding. The items that are about the site rather than the data, the stats page and the About page among them, are kept in the open-items note.

    • Measured
      Tell kept rows from moved rows
      Wherever submissions are drawn one by one, the strips and the proposed-start chart, a round dot is a row whose midpoints are where the quiz put them and an arrow is one that moved; the timeline and the factor panels do not make the distinction. The section on the proposed start, the level chart's second view, the bars beside each question, and the findings give the moved rows on their own. The recomputation checks out: all 107 kept rows, 106 without the one identical repeat, sit exactly where the page would have put them, and seven rows with three equal midpoints at a value no proposal produces are counted as moved.
    • Measured
      Store the proposed starting point with the submission
      The page has sent a calibration object with every quiz submission since 12 September, and 164 rows now carry it. On all 164, the stored proposal and the one the build script recomputes from the answers agree to within a tenth of a point. Its applied_at timestamp, stored for bookkeeping, turned out to be the source of this report's time-on-sliders finding.
    • Measured
      Refuse to register the same prediction twice
      Six identical repeat rows in the fortnight before the fix; none in the 222 rows since. Eleven browsers submitted more than once in that window and every one of them changed something, which is the case the fix was meant to let through.
    • Measured
      Reject rows without a level, and version the page
      All 222 rows since 2 September carry a level, a signature and a page version, across sixteen commits. No row without a level has arrived since.
    • Open
      Record where each visitor came from
      Still the most urgent item. 1 October was 37 rows at a median of 23%, the fortnight after it 58 rows at 54%, and two later days had medians under 25% on a handful of rows each. Whether those are the same source trickling on or new ones, nothing in any column says. Storing document.referrer, and a ?cohort= tag when the link carries one, as a diagnostic column outside the signed string would let the next report tie a burst to its source.
    • New
      Count slider interactions, not only the final position
      The time from proposal to submit sorts kept rows from moved ones better than any quiz answer does, but it cannot tell a visitor who agreed with the proposal from one who never engaged with it. Storing, outside the signed string, how many times each midpoint was dragged and whether it ever left the proposal before coming back would separate the two. It would also show whether the 44 movers who landed under 10% got there in one sweep or by working down. It costs one counter per slider.
    • New
      Ask check-takers to estimate their own score, and check a random sample of everyone
      The Dunning–Kruger effect is defined on the gap between how well people think they did and how well they did, and the site records only the second. One question after the check and before the verdict, "how many of the 30 do you think you got right?", stored with the row, would supply the first. And because only visitors who click the expert card are checked, the visitors who underrate themselves never appear; offering the check to a random tenth of beginner and medium choosers, after their quiz so it cannot change their path, would. With both, the next report could run the test properly, with the regression-to-the-mean correction the method needs.
    • Open
      Show the proposal to a random half
      Every statement here about the proposed number sets kept rows beside moved rows, which compares two kinds of visitor and not two treatments. The time finding makes the case sharper: a quick submitter keeps the number in front of them, so the number in front of them matters most for exactly the visitors who look at it least. Showing neutral sliders to a random half of quiz-takers, with the proposal stored but not applied and the arm stored in the row, is the one change that lets a report say "because". It is specified in the second kind of instrument.
    • Open
      Seed flagged decoys into all three self-selected quizzes
      Every list in the beginner and medium quizzes can only be answered correctly, so the only thing it measures is how many boxes someone ticks, and in this export the movers' thirds on the medium list changed order for the third report running. The beginner catastrophe list is ticked 89 to 91 times of 93 on its top four answers. The expert check shows what a decoy does. The case is set out in the note on the beginner quiz.
    • Open
      Add one recognition question about catastrophe pathways to the expert quiz
      The readers who pass the check put the last link of the chain at 50%, against 73% for beginners and 75% for medium takers, counting only visitors who set their own numbers. The expert quiz asks about mechanism and names and never mentions an outcome, so the cut could be rehearsal rather than belief. The note on it proposes one scored question; the version that would settle it shows the question to a random half of expert-quiz takers with the calibration rule held fixed.
    • Open
      Keep the expert quiz's reasoning questions, and watch them
      They still tell the two expert cohorts apart: 43 of 74 who passed the check ticked all four self-improvement answers, against 22 of 22 who declared themselves experts. The verified share has held at a little over half for three exports.
    • Open
      Ask one identical question at every level
      The three quizzes share no question, so the level lines in this report's new chart cannot be compared without the confound of three different question sets and three different proposals. One shared question would fix that. The beginner gut-reaction question sorts its cohort from 34% to 72% across three answers, and 27% to 48% among movers, and would be a candidate, if the aim is to compare rather than to test.
    • Open
      Stamp each submission with the option set it was shown
      The page version pins it exactly. What remains is teaching the build script to read the denominators from the stamp instead of the hand-kept start dates. This build had to be told by hand, again, that two new page versions carry the current term list.
    • Open
      Offer a one-minute survey after submission
      A psychologist's question, who submits 5% and who submits 90%, cannot be answered from quiz answers about films and vulnerabilities. The 2 September proposal picks eleven to thirteen items by hypothesis, places them after the submit button where they cannot cost a row, and stores them in their own table.

    These are directions the data supports, not a verdict on the design. Several rest on small cells: twelve people turned away by the check, sixteen October beginner movers, time bands of 21 to 42 rows, thirds of 11 to 32 people, and one day that still supplies 37 of the 424 rows.

    Visitor identity

    Since 2 September, not one identical repeat in 222 rows

    This section is bookkeeping for the people who run the site: it checks that the rows above are what they claim to be. Each browser holds a non-extractable P-256 key and signs a canonical string of its submission. The report verifies every signature against the public key the row carries, so "these rows came from one browser" is checked rather than asserted. What a key cannot show is that two different keys are two different people.

    A visitor who presses submit twice used to produce two rows. Since a fix on 2 September, none has.All six confirmed repeat rows in the table predate it, and every one of the 222 rows since is signed, verifies, and carries the commit the page was built from, across sixteen page versions. Eleven browsers submitted more than once in that window and every one of them changed something in between, which is the case the fix was meant to let through: three went beginner then medium, one of them thirteen days apart; two went beginner then cleared the check and took the expert quiz; one went medium then beginner; one took the medium quiz twice and then the expert one; and four took the same quiz more than once with the same answers and different sliders or bands. The one new browser since the last report is the last kind: on 2 October it took the beginner quiz, registered 52%, then moved the sliders twice more in the next six minutes and registered 19% and 20%. The 37 rows of 1 October come from 35 browsers. The rule looks at the whole payload, so it cannot catch a visitor who registers the same number twice with only an uncertainty band changed, and on its own terms it should not. All 253 signatures verify, and every field the signature covers agrees with the row: the p(doom) and its range, the three factors, the timestamp, the quiz level, the check score, the signing key and its counter. The signature does not cover the quiz answers, the terms ticked on the check, the proposed number, the time it was applied or the page version, so those columns are trusted, not checked. A key is a browser's signing identity, not a person: two keys can be one person, and one key is whoever holds that browser.
