The crowd thinned to a trickle. The numbers it left behind did not move.

477 anonymous submissions between 5 October 2025 and 19 November 2026, 53 of them new since the last report. Five quiet weeks are a chance to check which of the earlier findings were the crowd and which belong to the calculator. Most held. Two trends the last report was watching did not.

What a submission is

Each visitor sets three probabilities: that powerful AI arrives, that it then behaves dangerously, and that the danger then becomes a global catastrophe. The calculator multiplies them. The product is that visitor's p(doom).

Kept and moved

After a quiz the page does not show neutral sliders. It proposes a p(doom) from the answers and sets the sliders to it. Kept means the visitor registered that proposal with no midpoint changed. Moved means at least one was, and the arrow points the way the number went.

A kept row is the page's arithmetic. The moved rows are the nearest thing here to a visitor's own number, so the figures that matter are given for them separately.

A row is not a person

The unit is a submission. Since 19 August each one is signed with a per-browser key, so repeats from one browser can be told apart from two people agreeing; before that, nothing distinguishes them.

    Dunning–Kruger109 check-takers · 144 medium self-ratings

    Do the visitors who know least claim most? In what they tick, yes. In how sure they are, no.

    The Dunning–Kruger effect is the finding that people with the least skill overestimate it the most. Testing it properly needs a skill score that cannot be gamed and each person's own estimate of that score. The site has the first, for people who click the expert card, and not the second: nobody is asked how well they think they did.

    So this section does not draw the familiar chart of self-estimate against score by quartile, which takes the same shape from random numbers (Nuhfer and colleagues, 2016; Gignac and Zajenkowski, 2020). It asks three narrower questions the data can answer.

    What the data says

    Ten things this export supports, and how firmly

    1. The audience shrank and its middle did not move. 53 rows in 36 days against 58 in the 13 before. Median of the new rows 51%; October 50% on 117; September 49% on 130. Firm, for as long as "the median" is understood to include the kept rows.
    2. About a third of quiz-takers register the page's proposal unchanged. 127 of 367. By level: 40% of beginner rows, 40% of medium, 24% of expert. The kept rows' medians, 71%, 81% and 69%, are the formula's output. Firm as a level; the fortnight-to-fortnight share is noise.
    3. Speed predicts keeping. 67% of rows submitted within a minute kept the proposal, 10% of those that took three minutes or more, and the 53 new rows show the same step on their own. Firm. What it cannot say is whether a quick keeper agrees or is in a hurry.
    4. Movers go down, by a lot. 199 of 240 moved the number down; the median move is 30 points, and one mover in five ends under 10%. Firm.
    5. Take the proposal out and the three levels agree. Movers' medians: beginner 34%, medium 33%, expert 32%. Firm over the whole export; month by month the lines cross.
    6. The expert line's three-month fall has ended. 59%, 32%, 27%, then 37% on 13 rows. Tentative in both directions: the fall was real in the data, and so is the uptick, and neither rests on many rows.
    7. Verified experts cut the last link of the chain. Among movers who passed the check the chain runs 91%, 76%, 50%. Beginner and medium movers leave the last link at 71% and 75%. Firm as a description; whether it is a considered view or an expert quiz that never mentions an outcome is open.
    8. Passing the check goes with a lower number than claiming expertise did. 37% against 68% pooled, 29% against 44% among movers. The groups are months apart, so this is not the check's effect alone.
    9. What visitors recognise does not predict what they set. Among medium movers the knowledge questions correlate at zero. Thirds on every checklist reorder between exports, except the beginner catastrophe list (inverse) and the expert campaign-organisations list. Firm for the medium quiz.
    10. The rows are what they claim to be. All 306 signatures verify, and there has been no identical repeat in the 275 rows since the fix of 2 September. Firm.

    What to change next: the page can time a decision, and still cannot see one being made

    Each report ends with the changes the data argues for and marks what became of the earlier ones. Two changes to the page shipped in this window, the midpoint handle and a reworded beginner answer, and neither has enough rows behind it. The quiet weeks make one old item more urgent: with traffic this low, anything that depends on comparing fortnights will take months to read, so the changes worth making are the ones that add information to every row. Items about the site itself are in the open-items note.

    • Measured
      Tell kept rows from moved rows
      Every one-by-one chart draws them differently and every finding gives the moved rows on their own. All 128 kept rows sit exactly where the page would have put them.
    • Measured
      Store the proposal with the submission
      217 rows carry it, and on every one the stored and recomputed proposals agree to within 0.15 of a point. Its timestamp is the source of the time-on-sliders finding.
    • Measured
      Refuse to register the same prediction twice
      Six identical repeats in the fortnight before the fix; none in the 275 rows since. Fifteen browsers have submitted more than once in that window and every one changed something.
    • Measured
      Reject rows without a level, and version the page
      All 275 rows since 2 September carry a level, a signature and a page version, across twenty builds.
    • Shipped
      Give the midpoint a visible handle
      Live since 11 November. 15 rows, 4 kept. Leave it alone and read it at the next export, alongside the next item, without which it cannot really be read at all.
    • Shipped
      Qualify the beginner quiz's "easily shut down" answer
      Live since 7 November, seven beginner rows since. Nothing to report yet.
    • Open
      Count slider interactions, not only the final position
      Now the most useful single change. Time separates kept from moved rows better than any answer does, but it cannot tell a visitor who agreed with the proposal from one who never engaged with it, and it cannot say whether the new handle is being used. One counter per slider, stored outside the signed string, answers both on every row from the day it ships.
    • Open
      Record where each visitor came from
      The crowd of September and 1 October arrived and left, and nothing in any column says from where. Storing document.referrer, and a ?cohort= tag when the link carries one, as a diagnostic column would let the next burst be tied to its source.
    • Open
      Show the proposal to a random half
      Every statement here about the proposal sets kept rows beside moved rows, which compares two kinds of visitor and not two treatments. Neutral sliders for a random half, with the proposal stored but not applied, is the one change that lets a report say "because". It is specified in the second kind of instrument. At one and a half rows a day it would need months, which is an argument for starting it soon.
    • Open
      Ask check-takers to estimate their own score, and check a random sample of everyone
      The Dunning–Kruger effect is defined on the gap between how well people think they did and how well they did. One question before the verdict supplies the first half. Offering the check to a random tenth of beginner and medium choosers, after their quiz, supplies the people who underrate themselves.
    • Open
      Seed flagged decoys into the beginner and medium checklists
      Every list there can only be answered correctly, so it measures how many boxes someone ticks; the top four catastrophes are ticked 100 or 101 times out of 105. The expert check shows what a decoy adds. The case is in the note on the beginner quiz.
    • Open
      Add one question about catastrophe pathways to the expert quiz
      Verified experts put the last link at 50% where everyone else leaves it above 70%. The expert quiz asks about mechanisms and names and never mentions an outcome, so the cut could be rehearsal rather than belief. The note on it proposes one scored question shown to a random half.
    • Open
      Ask one identical question at every level
      The three quizzes share no question, so the level lines cannot be compared without three different question sets in the way. The beginner gut-reaction question sorts its cohort from 34% to 71% and would be a candidate.
    • Open
      Stamp each submission with the option set it was shown
      The page version pins it. What remains is teaching the build script to read denominators from the stamp. This build had to be told by hand, again, that four new page versions carry the current term list.
    • Open
      Offer a one-minute survey after submission
      Who submits 5% and who submits 90% cannot be answered from questions about films and vulnerabilities. The 2 September proposal places eleven to thirteen items after the submit button, where they cannot cost a row.

    These are directions the data supports, not a verdict on the design. Several rest on small cells: 16 people who fell short of the check, 15 rows since the handle shipped, 13 November experts, thirds of 13 to 37 people, and one evening that still supplies 37 of the 477 rows.

    Visitor identity

    Bookkeeping: every signature verifies, and no identical repeat in 275 rows

    This section is for the people who run the site. Each browser holds a non-extractable P-256 key and signs a canonical string of its submission, and the report verifies every signature against the public key the row carries. What a key cannot show is that two keys are two people.

    The 53 new rows come from 49 browsers, none of which had submitted before 15 October.Four browsers submitted twice. Two re-registered the same number within seconds with something else in the payload changed, which the duplicate rule lets through on purpose. One took the medium quiz and registered 19%, then cleared the check nine minutes later and registered 5% on the expert quiz. And on 19 November one expert kept the proposal at 69%, then two minutes later moved the sliders and registered 36%: a kept row and a moved row from the same person on the same quiz, the only such pair in the export. All 306 signatures verify, and every signed field agrees with its row. The signature does not cover the quiz answers, the terms ticked, the proposal, its timestamp or the page version, so those columns are trusted, not checked.
