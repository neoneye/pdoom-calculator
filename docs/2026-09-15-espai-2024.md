# The calculator against ESPAI 2024

**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Scope:** A comparison of the calculator's sliders, quizzes, exhibits, reports and
notes with Grace et al., *Advanced AI according to 1,580 researchers: uncertain,
unsafe, and sooner than we thought*
([PDF](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/ESPAI2024.pdf), September
2026), the fourth Expert Survey on Progress in AI. Calculator figures are from the
10 September export (253 rows), computed with the kept/moved rule in
`prepare_report_data.py`. Follows [the p(doom) literature](2026-09-13-pdoom-literature.md),
which cites the previous survey in the series.
**Outcome:** ESPAI moves the reference number the notes use from median 5% and mean
16% to median 10% and mean 18%. The calculator's visitors are well above that,
even counting only rows where they set the number themselves: median 31%, and 78%
at 10% or more against ESPAI's 51%. The broader definition explains part of that
gap. The sharpest difference is conditional. Assuming human-level AI exists, the
researchers put 10% on average on an extremely bad outcome, while the visitors'
second and third sliders multiply to 48%. The researchers also give AI in general a
higher risk (18%) than human-level AI in particular (10%), which a three-link chain
cannot express. The two instruments are strong in opposite places. ESPAI has a
sample anyone can describe, randomised wording and no number put in front of
anyone. The calculator records per link what each visitor was shown and what they
changed.

---

## 0. What ESPAI 2024 is

- **Who.** 1,580 researchers who published in 2023 at NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI,
  JMLR or IJCAI. 19,874 were emailed and the response rate was 10%. Each was
  offered $50.
- **When.** Fielded 9 to 24 December 2024 and published September 2026. The
  authors say themselves that it is over 18 months old.
- **How.** Blocks of questions are randomised, so most questions reach a third to a
  half of respondents. Wording is randomised too: fixed years against fixed
  probabilities, and three versions of the extinction question. The invitation
  describes the topic vaguely ("the future of AI progress") to limit
  self-selection on opinion.
- **Series.** It is the same instrument as 2016, 2022 and 2023. The literature
  note's "Grace et al. 2024, 2,778 AI authors, median 5%, mean ~16%" is the 2023
  wave (published in JAIR in 2025). This report is its successor.

## 1. The two instruments side by side

| | ESPAI 2024 | Calculator |
|---|---|---|
| Population | Authors at six AI venues, in any subfield or application domain | Anonymous visitors, self-selected |
| Familiarity measured | Self-reported thought about social impacts | Recognition quizzes; vocabulary check with decoys |
| Recruitment | Individual email; vague topic; paid | Whatever links reach the site; the topic is the site's name |
| Size | 1,580 (1,489 answered an extinction question) | 253 submissions, repeats included |
| Timing | One fortnight, December 2024 | Continuous since October 2025 |
| Outcome | "Human extinction or similarly permanent and severe disempowerment" | "Global catastrophe": extinction, disempowerment, collapse, s-risks |
| Horizon | None, or "within 100 years", by random assignment | None |
| Elicitation | One free-form probability | Three conditional sliders with bands, multiplied |
| Wording | Randomised variants, effects reported | One wording |
| Shown before answering | Definitions only | A quiz, then a proposed number already set on the sliders |
| Per-respondent record | Answers and demographics | Links, bands, quiz answers, check result, proposal, signature |
| Aggregation | Mean and median, with a methods appendix | Median, split by kept and moved |
| Data | Anonymised CSV published | Export in the repo |

## 2. The headline number, like for like

ESPAI reports the extinction answers as a mean, a median, an interquartile range
and the shares above thresholds. The same statistics on the calculator's cohorts:

| | n | Mean | Median | IQR | Zero | Under 1% | ≥ 10% | ≥ 25% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ESPAI, pooled variants** | 1,489 | 18.2% | 10% | 1–25% | 12% | 19% | 51% | 27% |
| ESPAI, generic wording | 744 | 18.3% | 10% | | | | 53% | 27% |
| ESPAI, loss of control | 392 | 18.5% | 9% | | | | 50% | 27% |
| ESPAI, within 100 years | 353 | 17.5% | 5% | | | | 49% | 26% |
| **Calculator, visitor-set** | 183 | 38.0% | 30.6% | 12–66% | 6% | 9% | 78% | 59% |
| no quiz, moved off 50/50/50 | 84 | 31.4% | 22.5% | 4–55% | 11% | 17% | 66% | 46% |
| quiz, moved a slider | 99 | 43.6% | 40.4% | 18–69% | 2% | 3% | 88% | 70% |
| quiz, verified expert, moved | 19 | 39.9% | 30.6% | 18–60% | 0% | 5% | 89% | 63% |
| Calculator, quiz, kept the proposal | 50 | 75.3% | 75.6% | 64–92% | 0% | 0% | 100% | 100% |

"Visitor-set" is every row whose number the visitor chose. That means quiz rows
where a midpoint was moved, plus rows without a quiz level that are not sitting on
the 50/50/50 defaults. Kept rows are the page's number and are left out of
the comparison. "Zero" and "under 1%" are the registered product, not a single slider.

Read with the definition in mind:

- **The median is three times ESPAI's.** "Global catastrophe" is wider than
  "extinction or severe disempowerment", so the calculator should run higher, and
  [the literature note](2026-09-13-pdoom-literature.md) said so. Against the old 5%
  median the gap was six-fold. Against 10% it is three-fold for visitor-set rows
  and about two-fold for the hand-set rows with no quiz.
- **The low tail is where the populations differ most.** One researcher in five
  puts extinction under 1%. On the calculator, one visitor-set row in eleven does,
  and among quiz-takers who moved a slider it is one in thirty-three. The hand-set
  rows with no quiz are the exception. Their zero rate, 11%, matches ESPAI's 12%.
  The quiz cohorts almost never reach it: three movers under 1%, two of them at zero.
- **The kept rows have no counterpart.** ESPAI never shows a respondent a number.
  Fifty of the calculator's 149 quiz rows are a number the page proposed and the
  visitor registered. Their 76% median is the one figure in the table that is
  not anyone's belief. It is kept out of the comparison, as
  [the stats-page note](2026-09-13-stats-page-kept-and-moved.md) keeps it out of
  the headline.

### What the horizon variant says about the undated question

[What the calculator is](2026-09-13-what-the-calculator-is.md) defends the missing
horizon as a design position. ESPAI gives the only direct measurement of the choice
in the literature. It randomly assigned the same question with and without "within
the next 100 years". Without the horizon the median doubles, from 5% to 10%, while
the mean barely moves, from 17.5% to 18.3%. So dropping the date moves the typical
respondent, not the pessimistic tail. Some of the calculator's distance from the
"by 2100" literature is the undated wording itself. ESPAI suggests that part is a
few points at the median, not tens.

## 3. The conditional question: where the chain and the survey disagree

ESPAI asks every respondent a second question shaped like the calculator's chain
after the first link. *Assume human-level AI (HLMI) will at some point exist*, then
split 100% across five long-run impacts (n = 1,538):

| Impact of HLMI | Mean | Median |
|---|---|---|
| Extremely good | 23.9% | 15% |
| On balance good | 27.7% | 25% |
| More or less neutral | 20.7% | 20% |
| On balance bad | 17.8% | 15% |
| Extremely bad (e.g. human extinction) | 9.9% | 5% |

The calculator's matching quantity is the second slider times the third, the
chance of catastrophe given powerful AI:

| | Mean of p₂·p₃ | Median of p₂·p₃ |
|---|---|---|
| ESPAI, "extremely bad" given HLMI | 9.9% | 5% |
| Calculator, visitor-set | 47.6% | 48.1% |
| no quiz, moved off defaults | 39.0% | 36.0% |
| verified experts, moved | 42.8% | 33.3% |

Three things follow.

**The gap is larger here than on the headline.** Four to five times the mean, and
seven to ten times the median. The condition cannot explain it. HLMI, "every task
better and more cheaply than human workers", is a stronger assumption than the first
slider's "strategic capability", so it should make ESPAI's conditional higher, not
lower. Two things plausibly do explain it. The response format makes respondents set
catastrophe against four other outcomes that must add to 100%. And "extremely
bad" is narrower than "global catastrophe".

**The researchers' two answers are not a chain.** Across the population, unconditional
extinction from "future AI advances" (mean 18%) exceeds extremely bad given HLMI
(mean 10%). ESPAI flags this and declines to read much into it, since different
assumptions and formats could produce it. But a chain cannot produce it at all. On the
calculator, p(doom) = p₁ · (p₂ · p₃) is never above p₂ · p₃. The researchers' pattern
fits one reading the calculator cannot hold: much of the risk comes from AI short of
human-level. That is the point
[the safety-argument draft](2026-09-14-making-the-safety-argument-explicit.md)
reached from the other side. A generally weak system can satisfy the first link
for a particular harm. ESPAI's numbers are some evidence that researchers already
reason that way.

**The format also shows ambivalence the sliders hide.** 51% of researchers put at
least 5% on both an extremely good and an extremely bad outcome. The calculator has
no good outcomes to put probability on, so a visitor who expects great benefit and
real risk can only express the risk.

## 4. Link by link

### First link: powerful AI

**ESPAI.** In the mean aggregate, HLMI reaches a 10% chance by 2027 and 50% by 2042,
five years earlier than the 2023 wave. The median aggregate reaches 50% by 2044.
Full automation of labour reaches 50% by 2098. The 50% date has moved about 3.4
years closer for each year since 2016. The authors think the survey
underweights industry researchers, whose timelines are anecdotally shorter. Wording
matters a lot: asking for probabilities at fixed years gives later dates than asking
for years at fixed probabilities.

**Calculator.** One undated slider. Its medians run from 0.76 on hand-set rows
without a quiz to 0.95 for verified experts. ESPAI never asks whether HLMI arrives
at all, only when, so the two numbers cannot be set against each other. What can
be said: ESPAI's shift of five years per year of survey is the argument for the
undated question at its strongest. A dated question asked in December 2024 is
already stale in 2026, and ESPAI's own limitations section says so.

**The Navier–Stokes exhibit.** ESPAI's footnote 7 names it. The task "solve a
long-standing math problem without human input, e.g. a Millennium Prize problem",
which respondents put at 50% by 2053, would not be satisfied by the September 2026
OpenAI solution, "without more input from humans" being part of the task. The
exhibit's own `limits` field agrees. Humans chose the problem, split it into
variants and used Codex to consolidate the results. [Where the zero goes](2026-09-13-where-the-zero-goes.md)
leans on this exhibit to argue that the first link is already close to one.
ESPAI's authors read the same event more narrowly, and the note should keep its
"harness, not a single LLM" qualification in front of the claim.

### Second link: dangerous behaviour

**ESPAI.** Asked how likely various traits are for at least some state-of-the-art
systems in 2044, more researchers than a year earlier said likely or very likely to:
being jailbroken into illegal actions, deceiving humans, having goals not aligned with
human goals, self-improving regardless of human wishes, and taking actions to attain
power. The last is still the trait fewest rate likely, but its median answer rose
from "unlikely" to "even chance". Only 22% think users of 2029's systems will be able
to know the true reasons for a decision. Stuart Russell's argument for AI risk is
rated important and harder than other problems, but only 12% place it among the
field's most important problems. Only 4% find the intelligence-explosion argument
"quite likely", down from 7–12% in earlier waves.

**Calculator.** The slider's explanation is "misaligned with human values", which is
ESPAI's "goals not aligned with human goals" almost word for word. The expert quiz's
self-improvement question ("Rewrite itself") and self-replication question cover the
traits ESPAI tracks. The medium quiz's speed question covers the fast-action
scenario: 57 of 62 answered that humans cannot stop a one-second financial attack in
time. The slider's medians run from 0.60 on hand-set rows to 0.80–0.87 among quiz
movers. The exhibits for this link (the Hugging Face intrusion, the AISI
supply-chain and social-engineering attempts, military targeting) all date from
2026, after ESPAI's fieldwork. The researchers were rating traits that the exhibits
now document in instances.

### Third link: global catastrophe

**ESPAI.** Asked which scenarios deserve concern over the next thirty years, 83% rate
AI-enabled misinformation such as deepfakes substantial or extreme. Next come
manipulation of public opinion, dangerous groups building powerful tools such as
engineered viruses, authoritarian control, and economic inequality. The authors
add a limitation: their extinction question bundles disempowerment in, so it is not
clear how many researchers fear extinction itself. The loss-of-control variant
(median 9%) scored about the same as the generic one (10%). The authors take this,
weakly, as a sign that "inability to control" describes much of what respondents
have in mind.

**Calculator.** The third slider is the containment question, and loss of control is
also the beginner quiz's gut-reaction question. 30 of 46 beginners chose "it would
likely outsmart our attempts to stop it", 13 "manageable", 3 "easily shut down". The
beginner capability list overlaps ESPAI's scenario list in propaganda, mass
surveillance and warfare. The biorisk-uplift exhibit is ESPAI's "dangerous groups…
engineered viruses" scenario, measured. Misinformation and deepfakes, the
researchers' top concern, appear nowhere in the quizzes or exhibits except as
"propaganda". That fits the site's scope: deepfakes are a harm, but rarely a global
catastrophe. It is still worth knowing that the site's third link and the
researchers' thirty-year worries point at different things.

## 5. Who knows more, and which way they move

**ESPAI.** Researchers who had thought more about the social impacts of
smarter-than-human AI gave somewhat higher risk. Every group except the least
attentive had the same median, 10%. Those who did their undergraduate studies in Asia gave
more risk than those from North America or Europe. Three quarters think public
concern about AI risk is at least somewhat due to misunderstanding AI research.

**Calculator.** Among visitors who moved a slider, the verified experts are lowest
(median 31%), below self-declared experts (44%) and beginners (48%). They get there by
cutting the third link to 0.50 ([experts cut the last link](2026-09-13-experts-cut-the-last-link.md)).

These point in opposite directions, and they measure different things. ESPAI's
axis is self-reported attention to risk. The calculator's axis is a vocabulary check
with decoys. Neither contradicts the other. Together they say that "informed" is
not one variable. Attention to the risk arguments goes with more concern.
Familiarity with the vocabulary of current systems, among visitors who chose to set
their own number, goes with less concern about the last link.

### A published AI researcher is not the calculator's expert

On this site an expert is someone with hands-on experience of machine learning, who
knows how to make these systems solve problems. They have written system prompts,
wired up tools and watched a model go wrong. A philosophy professor who has never
written a system prompt is not an expert in this sense, however much they have
published about AI. The expert check only approximates this definition. It tests
vocabulary, which people with hands-on experience have, but which can also be read
up on.

ESPAI's respondents qualify by where they published, not by what they know. Six
venues is a wide net. AAAI and IJCAI cover planning, logic, constraint solving and
game theory. NeurIPS takes computational neuroscience and cognitive science. Many
papers also have co-authors whose contribution was the application domain: a
psychologist, a clinician, a physicist. All of them are "AI researchers" by ESPAI's
rule. Many would have no reason to know what a system prompt, an embedding or a
tool call is, and nothing in the survey asks.

Timing widens the gap. The qualifying papers are from 2023 and the fieldwork is
December 2024, before agent harnesses, tool calling and prompt injection were
everyday vocabulary. The calculator's medium quiz and expert check ask about
exactly that vocabulary. So the two "expert" labels overlap without either
containing the other. A 2023 NeurIPS author in cognitive science could fail the
expert check. A developer who runs coding agents all day could pass it without
ever having published.

This changes how ESPAI's number should be used here. It is not "what the experts
think", set against the calculator's non-experts. It is what a broad, describable
research community thinks. Its familiarity measure is attention to social impacts,
not hands-on knowledge of the systems the second link is about. The comparison
that would test the calculator's verified cohort is a survey of people who build
and deploy current systems, and ESPAI's authors say theirs underweights them.

Education is not awareness either. The author has watched well-educated friends
dismiss the risk as something that can never happen. ESPAI has a version of this
among its own respondents. Only the group that had given the social impacts least
thought had a median below 10%, and 12% of all respondents put the risk at exactly
zero. The survey cannot say whether those zeros are considered views or topics
never thought about. What it does show is that a publication record does not
guarantee someone has thought about the question.

ESPAI's limitations section adds a caution that applies to the calculator's
"verified" label as much as to its own sample: expertise is a poor predictor of
forecasting accuracy.

## 6. The opinion questions that line up

| Question | ESPAI 2024 | Calculator |
|---|---|---|
| Safety research funding | 72% say prioritise "more" (36%) or "much more" (36%), n = 748 | Expert quiz: 29 of 41 (71%) "we are not spending enough"; 6 defensive technologies; 3 stop all AI research; 2 capabilities; 1 about right |
| Pace of progress | 34% faster, 29% current, 34% slower; "much slower" up from 5% to 9% | Medium quiz: competition degrades safety 42 of 62, improves it 2; regulation cannot keep up 43 of 62 |

The funding figures agree almost exactly. They are not independent evidence,
though. On the calculator the funding answer picks the p(doom) band the quiz
proposes, and the options differ. On pace, ESPAI's researchers split evenly while
the calculator's medium quiz-takers are one-sided. The questions are not the same:
competition and regulation are not speed. But the direction is clear enough that a
visitor-set 31% median should not be surprising.

## 7. Method: what each does that the other does not

**ESPAI has, and the calculator lacks:**

- **A describable sample and a stated recruitment rule.** The calculator's weakness,
  as every note already says. The sample is described by publication venue,
  though, not by knowledge of current systems (section 5).
- **Topic-blind recruitment.** ESPAI hides its subject in the invitation. The
  calculator's subject is its name, so it gets the most self-selection on opinion
  a sample can have.
- **Randomised wording, with the effect reported.** The one-wording caution in the
  literature note now has numbers behind it. Changing the horizon doubles the
  median, and changing the timeline wording shifts dates by years.
- **No proposed number.** ESPAI's cleaning deals with confused answers: 331
  responses removed because probabilities fell over time, mostly shares treated as
  summing to 100%. The calculator's sliders cannot produce that error. They
  produce the proposal effect instead, a different contamination measured on the
  same rows.
- **Aggregation compared openly.** An appendix shows how mean against median, and
  one fitting loss against another, move the headline. The calculator's stats page
  still leads with a mean, 43.4%, which
  [the stats-page note](2026-09-13-stats-page-kept-and-moved.md) proposes replacing.

**The calculator has, and ESPAI lacks:**

- **Per-link numbers**, which let the conditional in section 3 be computed at all.
- **A record of what each respondent saw, and whether they moved it.** ESPAI can
  argue about its non-response bias. The calculator measured its own priming, 50 of
  149 rows.
- **Continuity.** ESPAI is a snapshot published 21 months after fieldwork, and the
  calculator's rows are days old. Given ESPAI's own finding that timelines shorten
  by years per year, that matters more than it would for a stable topic.

## 8. What this updates in the existing notes

Factual updates only. None of this calls for a change to the page.

- **[The p(doom) literature](2026-09-13-pdoom-literature.md):** the Grace row
  describes the 2023 wave. The current figures are 1,580 researchers, median 10%,
  mean 18.2%, 51% at 10% or more. The claim that the no-quiz cohort "lines up with
  Grace once the definition is broader" still holds. Its median (15% there, 13.5%
  on all 103 level-less rows) sits just above ESPAI's 10%, and below ESPAI's 18% mean.
- **[What the calculator is](2026-09-13-what-the-calculator-is.md):** "Grace for the
  reference number" on a future About page should use 10%. ESPAI's within-100-years
  variant is the empirical answer to how much the missing horizon costs.
- **[Where the zero goes](2026-09-13-where-the-zero-goes.md):** ESPAI's authors
  explicitly decline to count the Navier–Stokes result as a Millennium problem solved
  "without human input". The note's case that the first link is near one depends
  on its harness framing, and should say so first.
- **[Experts cut the last link](2026-09-13-experts-cut-the-last-link.md):** ESPAI's
  attention axis runs the other way from the calculator's vocabulary axis. This is
  a reason not to generalise the verified-expert finding beyond "people who pass this
  check".
